Russian word order is much freer than English, because the case endings, not the position, show who does what. Мама любит папу and Папу любит мама both say that mum loves dad. But the order still matters: it shows what the sentence is about, and what is new.

## Known first, new last

In a neutral sentence, Russian puts what is already known (the topic, **тема**) first, and the new information (**рема**) last, at the end of the sentence, where the main stress falls.

## Pronouns usually go before the verb

Unstressed object pronouns usually come before the verb: я тебя люблю, я его знаю. After the verb (я люблю тебя) is also correct, and puts a little more weight on the pronoun.

## Questions

A yes/no question usually keeps the word order of a statement; only the voice changes, rising sharply on the word being asked about. Ты читал эту книгу? can ask whether it was *you*, whether you *read* it, or whether it was *this* book, depending on where the voice rises. In writing, you can make it clear by putting that word first and adding ли (see Particles).

## Some fixed habits

- Adjectives go before the noun: новая книга. After the noun (книга новая), the adjective becomes the point: *the book is new*.
- очень and other words that strengthen go right before the word they strengthen: я тебя очень люблю.
- не goes right before the word it negates: я не сделал это (*I didn't do it*), but не я сделал это (*it wasn't me who did it*).

## Common mistakes

| Not this | But this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Книга лежит на столе. (to say *there's a book on the desk*) | На столе лежит книга. | Correct, with another meaning: it's about *the* book, one we already know. |
| Я написал эту книгу. (answering *who wrote this book?*) | Эту книгу написал я. | Correct, but unnatural as an answer: the new information (я) goes last. |
| Я тебя люблю очень. | Я тебя очень люблю. | Unnatural: очень goes before the word it strengthens. |
