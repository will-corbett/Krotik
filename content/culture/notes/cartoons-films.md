A handful of Soviet cartoons and films are known by almost everyone, across generations and across the countries where Russian is spoken. Their lines turn up in everyday conversation, so knowing them helps you catch jokes and references.

## Cartoons

| Cartoon | What it is |
|---|---|
| Ну, погоди! | a wolf forever chasing a hare, almost without words |
| Винни-Пух | the Soviet Winnie-the-Pooh, with its own songs and jokes |
| Чебурашка | a furry creature of unknown species and his friend, the crocodile Гена |
| Ёжик в тумане | a short, dreamlike film about a hedgehog lost in fog |

Most of these are on the studio's official Soyuzmultfilm channel (see the links on the Culture page). They are short, and many are simple enough for beginners: Ну, погоди! has almost no words, Винни-Пух and Чебурашка speak slowly and clearly.

## Modern cartoons

Newer series are just as useful for learners: Smeshariki (round animal friends), Fixiki (tiny people who explain how things work), Маша и Медведь (a lively girl and a patient bear). Each has an official channel, and the episodes are short.

## Films

New Year's Eve on television traditionally means a few comedies from the 1960s and 1970s, which people quote all year round. Comedies are a good first choice: the plots are easy to follow, and the language is everyday speech.

A good way to start:

1. Pick a comedy or a children's film on a studio's own channel; the Odesa and Gorky studios put many online for free.
2. Watch with Russian subtitles if they exist, or with English ones the first time.
3. Watch a favourite scene again without subtitles, and note a few phrases.

> Watch on the studios' own channels, not on re-uploads: it's free, legal and usually better quality.
